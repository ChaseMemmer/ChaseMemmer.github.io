About Me


Imaginative. Knowledgeable. Driven.

A multidisciplinary professional who can handle all aspects of hardware and related software. Highly motivated to leave a lasting mark on the industry.

  • Career Goals

    I'm aiming to work in Research and Development for a company specializing in mechatronics/robotics. Ideally, this company would produce prosthetics, exoskeletons or other human-augmentation technology.

AEVEX Aerospace

July 2024 – Present

At AEVEX, I moved into the kind of engineering environment I like most: small teams, ambiguous requirements, real hardware, and fast ownership. I have served as a subsystem lead or sole electrical engineer across multiple projects, owning architecture, requirements, design reviews, validation planning, and production tradeoffs from early concept toward buildable systems. The work has included drone systems, launcher architecture, long-range communications hardware, production-focused redesign, and DFx improvements. The common thread has been learning how to turn a vague technical need into something that can be built, tested, reviewed, and shipped to a customer without falling apart.

The House Project

October 2024 – Present

I bought a fixer-upper and accidentally gave myself a second engineering curriculum. Old houses are full of practical systems problems: electrical, plumbing, drywall, moisture, structure, old hardware, budget constraints, and previous decisions that made sense to someone at the time. It has been a good reminder that real systems are messy, documentation is rare, and the best solution is usually the one that works safely, affordably, and today.

M.S. in Mechanical Engineering

June 2025

I finished my master's degree in mechanical engineering after previously completing bachelor's degrees in both mechanical engineering and electrical engineering. That combination shaped how I think. I am most comfortable when mechanical constraints, electrical architecture, controls, software, and manufacturing concerns are all tangled together. Real products rarely respect department boundaries, and I have always liked that.

Master's Research

January 2024 – June 2025

For my master's research, I studied EMG-based control methods for biomimetic prosthetic systems. The project sat at the intersection of controls, signal processing, biomechanics, and hardware design. The core challenge was turning noisy biological signals into useful control intent — the kind of problem where the math matters, but so do sensors, hardware limitations, timing, usability, and the fact that human bodies are not clean laboratory systems. This project helped pull together a lot of threads I had been building separately: robotics, controls, embedded thinking, mechanical design, and the desire to make hardware that interacts with people instead of just sitting on a bench.

NH Mountain Hiking

While working at DEKA in Manchester, I spent weekends exploring New Hampshire's mountains. It was a good counterweight to medical-device R&D: long technical weeks followed by long trails, steep climbs, bad weather, and the occasional reminder that stubbornness is a transferable skill.

DEKA

January 2023 – July 2024

DEKA was where I learned what it means to be the electrical owner inside a large multidisciplinary R&D effort. I was the sole electrical engineer on a 35-person medical-device team, responsible for power and signal design decisions across panels, harnesses, PCBAs, controls interfaces, verification, and compliance work. The job required technical depth, but also a lot of translation: mechanical constraints, software needs, regulatory requirements, test realities, and system-level goals all had to meet somewhere. That "somewhere" was often electrical.

ABEX Satellite Project

January 2022 – January 2023

I led a small student team designing the energy management system for the ABEX satellite project. The system included power optimization, energy storage, battery charge/discharge control, DC-DC conversion, safety considerations, and communication with the broader satellite architecture. My role covered hardware design, part selection, budgeting, documentation, and keeping the project moving when the interfaces were still changing. It was one of my first real experiences with the difference between designing a circuit and designing a subsystem.


DEA Project

February 2022 – May 2022

For a semester project, I researched, modeled, and built a modular dielectric elastomer actuator system. The project involved LTSpice modeling, KiCad design, oscillator and current-amplifier stages, voltage multiplication, and a final operating voltage around 1600 V. It was a useful mix of theory, circuit design, high-voltage caution, and the very practical lesson that "simple actuator" does not always mean simple electronics.

SCARA Pick-n-Place Robot

January 2021 – May 2021

I built a small SCARA-style pick-and-place robot with a friend as a learning project. We modified a base design, rebuilt parts in SolidWorks, 3D printed much of the structure, and controlled the system using Python and Arduino. The project involved forward and inverse kinematics, motor control, mechanical packaging, and the usual debugging that comes from asking software, electronics, and plastic parts to agree with each other.


Quincy Compressor Co-Op

May 2019 – May 2020

At Quincy Compressor, I worked in the R&D lab supporting compressor testing, lab procedures, data analysis, calibration, contractor coordination, and compliance work. It was an early introduction to industrial engineering reality: test equipment matters, procedures matter, standards matter, and small measurement problems can become expensive if nobody owns them. I also got my first real taste of engineering work that had to support products, labs, technicians, managers, vendors, and deadlines at the same time.


Robotic Mining Competition

September 2018 – May 2020

NASA Robotic Mining was the project that pulled me deeply into hands-on engineering. I worked on mechanical design for a mining robot built to excavate simulated icy regolith under competition constraints. Over three years, I moved from contributor to technical lead, helped redesign major mechanisms, modeled and simulated large assemblies in SolidWorks, and contributed to a robot that won at the national level. It was also where I learned one of my favorite engineering lessons: a clever design is only useful if it survives contact with dirt, rules, time, tired students, and the repair table.


Ntnl Space Grant Conference

February 2020

After the University of Alabama's success in NASA Robotic Mining, our team was selected to represent Alabama at the National Space Grant Conference in Washington, D.C. I attended as a technical lead and helped present the project to people from NASA, government, academia, and the broader space-technology community. It was an early lesson that building the thing is only half the job. At some point, you also have to explain why it matters.
